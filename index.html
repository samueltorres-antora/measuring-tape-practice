How a ruler works
Understanding inches, halves, quarters, eighths & sixteenths
Foundation
1
Every inch is divided into 16 equal parts
The distance between two whole numbers (e.g. 0″ and 1″) is split into 16 tiny slices. Each slice is 1/16″. All other fractions are just groups of those slices.
2
Tick height = fraction importance
The ruler uses height as a shortcut — you don't need to count every tiny line. Taller ticks mark bigger fractions. Learn the 5 heights and you can read any position instantly.
Whole inch — tallest, printed number
Half inch — 2nd tallest, easy to spot
Quarter inch — medium height
Eighth inch — short
Sixteenth inch — shortest
3
Use landmarks, not counting
The fastest readers navigate like a map. Find the nearest big landmark (whole inch → half → quarter), then count tiny ticks from there. Example: to find 7/16″, find the ½″ mark and step left one tiny tick — that's it.

Reading fractions on a ruler
Step-by-step method for any measurement
Technique
1
Start at the nearest whole inch to the left
Look at the measurement point. Find the closest whole-inch number that is at or to the left of it. That's your base. Example: if a point is between 2″ and 3″, your base is 2″.
2
Find the nearest landmark between that inch and the point
Look for the tall ½″ tick first. Is the point before or after it? Then narrow to ¼ or ¾. Use the height of the nearest tick to place yourself quickly.
3
Count tiny (sixteenth) ticks from the landmark
Once you're oriented to the nearest quarter or half, count the short ticks left or right to reach the exact point. Each tiny tick = 1/16″.
Worked example — read this point
①Arrow is between 0″ and 1″ — base is 0″
②Arrow is past the ½ mark, before the ¾ mark — between ½ and ¾
③Count from ¾ going left: arrow is 1 tiny tick before ¾
✓Answer: 11/16″ (¾ minus 1/16)

Adding measurements
How to add two lengths on a tape measure
Prep for Quiz 2
1
Convert everything to sixteenths first
Fractions are easiest to add when they share a common unit. Since the smallest division is 1⁄16″, convert each measurement: 1″ = 16⁄16, ½″ = 8⁄16, ¼″ = 4⁄16, ⅛″ = 2⁄16, 1⁄16 = 1⁄16.
1″= 16 sixteenths
½″= 8 sixteenths
¼″= 4 sixteenths
⅛″= 2 sixteenths
1⁄16″= 1 sixteenth
2
Add the sixteenths, then simplify
Add the two sixteenth counts together, then convert back. If the total is 16 or more, divide by 16 to get whole inches. Simplify any leftover fraction if possible.
Worked example — ¾″ + ⅝″
①¾″ = 12 sixteenths
②⅝″ = 10 sixteenths
③12 + 10 = 22 sixteenths
④22 ÷ 16 = 1 remainder 6 → 1 and 6⁄16
✓Simplified: 1⅜″ (6⁄16 = ⅜)

Subtracting measurements
How to find what remains after cutting or trimming
Prep for Quiz 2
1
The same sixteenths trick works for subtraction
Convert both measurements to sixteenths, subtract, then convert back. This avoids any fraction confusion — it's just plain number subtraction.
2
Real-world use: cutting a piece to length
You have a board that measures 2¼″. You need to cut 11⁄16″ off one end. What's left? Convert both to sixteenths, subtract, convert back.
Worked example — 1½″ − 5⁄16″
①1½″ = 1×16 + 8 = 24 sixteenths
②5⁄16″ = 5 sixteenths
③24 − 5 = 19 sixteenths
④19 ÷ 16 = 1 remainder 3 → 1 and 3⁄16
✓Answer: 1 3⁄16″

How the metric ruler works
Centimetres, millimetres and the tick hierarchy
Foundation
1
The base unit is the millimetre (mm)
On a metric ruler the smallest division is 1 mm. Ten millimetres make one centimetre (cm). So every cm span contains exactly 10 mm ticks.
1 cm= 10 mm
5 mm= 0.5 cm
1 mm= 0.1 cm
25.4 mm= 1 inch

Full ruler — every 5 mm labelled

Tick reference — 1 mm to 5 mm

2
Three tick heights — simpler than inches
Metric rulers have only three tiers, making them quicker to read than fractional inch rulers.
Full cm — tallest, printed number (1, 2, 3…)
5 mm — medium height, halfway between cm marks
1 mm — shortest, 9 of these between each cm
3
Use the 5 mm tick as your landmark
Just like the ½″ mark on an inch ruler, the 5 mm tick is your key landmark. Find the nearest cm number, check if the point is before or after the 5 mm mark, then count individual mm ticks.

Reading mm and cm measurements
Step-by-step method for any metric measurement
Technique
1
Find the nearest cm number to the left
Look at the measurement point and find the printed cm number just to its left. That is your whole-cm base. Example: a point between the 3 and 4 cm marks has a base of 3 cm.
2
Check the 5 mm landmark
Is the point before or after the medium-height tick halfway between the two cm marks? That tick is the 5 mm mark. If the point is past it, your sub-cm count starts at 5.
3
Count remaining 1 mm ticks
Count the short ticks from the nearest landmark to your point. Add them to your base. Each short tick = 1 mm.
Worked example — read the arrow
①Arrow is between 2 cm and 3 cm — base is 2 cm = 20 mm
②Arrow is before the 5 mm landmark — so less than 25 mm
③Count 3 short (1 mm) ticks from the 2 cm mark
✓Answer: 23 mm (or 2.3 cm)

Reading measurements above 1 inch
Whole numbers + fractions = mixed numbers
Foundation
1
Read the whole inches first
Find the largest printed whole number that is at or to the left of your measurement point. That is your whole-inch base. A point between the 2 and 3 marks has a base of 2″.
2
Read the fraction the same way as before
From the whole-inch mark, use the same landmark method — find ½, then ¼ or ¾, then count tiny ticks. The fraction you get is the part to add to your whole number.
3
Combine: whole + fraction = your measurement
Write the whole number first, then the fraction. 2 + 3/8 = 2⅜″. This is called a mixed number. Every measurement on a tape measure beyond 1″ is a mixed number.
4
The fraction pattern repeats every inch
The tick heights between 2″ and 3″ are identical to those between 0″ and 1″. Once you learn one inch, you can read any inch on the tape.

Step-by-step for three common measurements
Technique

①Arrow is between 1″ and 2″ — whole number base is 1″
②Arrow is past the 1½ mark — in the second quarter of the inch
③Arrow is on the ¾ landmark tick exactly
✓Answer: 1¾″ (1 whole inch + ¾)
Example 2 — read this measurement
①Arrow is between 1″ and 2″ — whole number base is 1″
②Arrow is before the 1½ mark, past the 1¼ mark
③Count 1 tiny tick past the ¼ landmark → that is 5/16
✓Answer: 1 5/16″ (1 whole inch + ¼ + 1 sixteenth)

③One eighth-height tick past the ¾ mark = ⅞
✓Answer: 3⅞″ (3 whole inches + ⅞)

Improper fractions on a ruler
When the numerator is larger than the denominator
Advanced
1
What is an improper fraction?
A fraction where the top number (numerator) is bigger than the bottom number (denominator). Examples: 5/4, 19/8, 27/16. These represent measurements larger than 1 inch.
5/4bigger than 1 (4/4)
9/8bigger than 1 (8/8)
19/8bigger than 2 (16/8)
27/16bigger than 1 (16/16)
2
How to convert to a mixed number
Divide the numerator by the denominator. The quotient is the whole number. The remainder over the denominator is the fraction part.
5/45 ÷ 4 = 1 remainder 1 → 1 1/4″
19/819 ÷ 8 = 2 remainder 3 → 2 3/8″
27/1627 ÷ 16 = 1 remainder 11 → 1 11/16″
38/1638 ÷ 16 = 2 remainder 6 → 2 6/16 = 2 3/8″
3
Everything is in sixteenths on a tape measure
A tape measure uses sixteenths as its smallest division. To convert any measurement to sixteenths and back, remember: 1″ = 16/16, so multiply whole inches by 16 and add any fraction already in sixteenths.
2 3/4″= (2×16 + 12)/16 = 44/16
3 5/16″= (3×16 + 5)/16 = 53/16
1 7/8″= (1×16 + 14)/16 = 30/16
Example — where is 19/8″ on the ruler?
①Convert: 19 ÷ 8 = 2 remainder 3 → 2 and 3/8
②Find the 2″ mark on the ruler
③Count 3 eighth-ticks past the 2″ mark
✓19/8″ = 2 3/8″ — the arrow shows exactly where it lands
Example — where is 27/16″ on the ruler?
①Convert: 27 ÷ 16 = 1 remainder 11 → 1 and 11/16
②Find the 1″ mark, then find the ¾ landmark (12/16)
③Step back 1 tiny sixteenth tick from ¾ → that is 11/16
✓27/16″ = 1 11/16″
Example — convert a measurement TO an improper fraction
You read 2 3/4″ on the ruler. What is it as a single fraction over 16?
①Convert whole inches: 2″ = 2 × 16 = 32 sixteenths
②Convert fraction: ¾ = 12/16 = 12 sixteenths
③Add: 32 + 12 = 44 sixteenths
✓2 3/4″ = 44/16″
Why this matters: When adding or subtracting measurements in math (like in Quiz 3), converting to a single fraction over 16 makes the arithmetic much easier before converting back.
